The Problem Gate

The rule thirty projects copied in weeks, its own author abandoned in twelve days, and nobody bothered to name.

Here's what a denial-of-service attack looks like in 2026: nobody crashes your server. They send you help.

Ask the curl project, which runs on roughly everything with a power button. Its bug bounty spent years paying strangers to report real vulnerabilities — until generated reports, polite and plausible and wrong, swelled to about a fifth of the queue while real finds sank below five percent. In January the bounty died. In July, the maintainers spent a month accepting no reports at all. Not because attackers won. Because helpers did.

Same story everywhere you look. arXiv stopped accepting unreviewed survey papers in computer science — moderators were drowning in hundreds of generated ones a month. Wikipedia built a fast-track deletion rule because deliberation can't match generation. The Jazzband Python collective shut down. GitHub shipped maintainers a kill switch for outside pull requests. One formula under all of it, gorgeous in its ugliness: creating became free; reviewing didn't. Every process whose front door accepts finished work is now a target, and yours is on the list.

Two sentences in a policy file

On January 22, 2026, Mitchell Hashimoto put two sentences into the Ghostty terminal's contribution policy: "Pull requests created in any way by AI can only be for accepted issues. Drive-by pull requests that do not reference an accepted issue will be closed."

Then the copying started — not parallel invention, literal template adoption. Databuddy the next day. The Fedify ecosystem, about fifteen repos, two days later. Polars. Delve. NetExec's commit message has the beautiful laziness of a man reaching for a fire extinguisher: "Add original ghostty AI policy." A code search finds Ghostty's sentence in roughly fifty-six repositories. A parallel template by Will McGugan spread alongside it, and LangChain universalized the rule for all outside contributions, human or machine, with the most honest sentence any policy file has produced this year: "Mass automated contributions represent a denial-of-service attack on our human effort." The hapi.js project wrote the best gloss of what "accepted" means: the problem is real, the approach has been discussed, and a human on the team has already decided the work is worth doing.

So what did we collectively call this thing that thirty-odd projects adopted in a few months? Nothing. The practitioners copied sentences like medieval scribes, no theory attached. The academics filed it in a taxonomy — twelve governance strategies, and this one sits in a drawer labeled "Scope and Intentionality Control". Try rallying a movement behind that. A rule this copied, this fast, with no name, is a fire spreading through buildings that don't have a word for "firewall."

Your gate is older than your programming language

Here's what none of the adopters mentioned, probably because none of them knew: this rule has been running serious institutions for decades.

The IETF won't move your draft toward a standard until a working group adopts it — accepts it as the group's problem. Python has required an accepted PEP for major changes since 2000. Rust wrote it as a warning in 2014: submit a feature without an RFC and it "may be closed with a polite request to submit an RFC first." Kubernetes' release machinery refuses feature graduation without a KEP. Godot's main tracker literally refuses feature requests — problems go to a separate proposals repo and wait for acceptance. Remember Godot; it comes back. OpenJDK has a bot that won't integrate your change unless it resolves to a tracked issue.

Outside software it's older and cleaner. More than 300 scientific journals now practice Registered Reports: they accept your question and method before any results exist. Grant funding runs this way by construction — the funder writes the problem, and solutions compete inside it.

But notice the scope. Every one of those gates guarded only the big stuff: substantial changes, major features, public APIs. Small fixes flowed freely everywhere, and that was rational — when writing code was expensive, an unsolicited patch was a gift, and the gate existed to protect the contributor from wasting months on something nobody wanted.

The 2026 gate is the same door with the opposite reason. Nobody is protecting the contributor's effort anymore; the effort is free. The gate now exists because a free solution is itself a cost — each one arrives with a review bill attached, payable by you. Same mechanism, inverted economics, and nobody named the flip.

So let's name it. The problem gate: no solution enters until a human has accepted the problem it claims to kill.

Not an AI ban — Ghostty's own policy says the project is written "with plenty of AI assistance." Not a disclosure rule — disclosure tells you how the sausage was made and changes nothing about the pile of sausages. Not a fence — rate limits and trust tiers cut volume. The problem gate changes the type: your front door stops accepting answers and starts accepting questions. Volume was never the disease. The artifact was.

The author defected. Good.

Twelve days after writing the most-copied contribution rule of the year, Hashimoto replaced it. Ghostty's current rules gate people: a first-time contributor opens a discussion, a maintainer vouches for them, and unvouched submissions close automatically. Gate the person, not the problem. So the year's most-copied rule is one its own author already considers insufficient — and as far as we can tell, nobody has publicly asked which gate is right. Let's actually ask.

Vouching works. Private clubs always work. And you should be honest about what it costs, because the cost is the club: trust caps at acquaintance, the stranger with the real bug is back outside the wall, and "open contribution" quietly becomes alumni-only. For one exhausted project that's a fine trade. As the default shape of working together, it's the end of the thing that made openness worth having.

And the problem gate has its own embarrassment, so let's say it out loud instead of hiding it: problems flood too. curl's slop was problem-shaped — a fake vulnerability report is a fake problem, and it burned the maintainers anyway. A bare problem gate just relocates the pile from one inbox to another.

The gate earns its keep only when the problem is typed: what hurts, who it hurts, what it costs to ignore, and how you'll know it's gone. That shape does two things a solution can never do. It's checkable against shared reality — does it reproduce, does anyone actually bleed, would anyone notice if it vanished — which makes triage cheap in exactly the way reviewing a five-hundred-line diff is not. And accepting one is an agenda decision, not a code review: a human saying "yes, this is worth anyone's time," before any particular answer exists to get attached to.

That's the theory of the unit, and it fits in two sentences. You can verify a problem against the world. You can verify a person only against your address book. One of those scales to strangers — and strangers were the entire point.

The other answer: shoot the robots

While the gate was spreading by copy-paste, the two flagship open-source game engines reached for something blunter. Bevy declared all AI-generated contributions unmergeable — code, textures, audio, everything. Godot, drowning in more than 4,600 open pull requests, went next: in June 2026 it banned autonomous agents and vibe coding outright, allowing AI only for "menial things," with unappealable bans for violators. A third gate, then. Not the problem, not the person — the provenance: how the work was made. It even has its own awards list.

Here's how that's going. Bevy's ban met the mirror test and lost: by summer, its own policy appendix admitted "we could not fairly and reliably enforce or adjudicate this policy" — silent use was invisible, accusations did damage whether true or not, and honest people faced "a strong incentive to defect." The total ban quietly retreated to disclosure. And Godot? Godot is the project that has run a real problem gate for features since 2019 — the proposals repo from the genealogy above. Its new policy doesn't mention that gate once. The most successful problem-gate operator in software met the flood and reached for an origin ban and a tenure rule instead. Maybe they judged their flood differently. Or maybe you can't generalize a mechanism nobody ever named. You cannot reach for a word that doesn't exist.

So say the unit theory again, with all three gates on the table. A problem you can check against the world. A person you can check against your address book. Provenance you can check against nothing but vibes — Bevy's appendix is the signed confession.

One concession before moving on, because Godot's maintainers are right about their own project: no gate makes reviewing a wanted fix cheaper. "PR review is the largest bottleneck in the engine right now," their policy says, and their co-founder's honest cure is money for reviewers, not rules. The problem gate won't fund anyone. What it does is narrower, and prior: it guarantees that every expensive review answers something a human already asked for. First stop reviewing what nobody wanted. The bill for the rest is a real problem — and it deserves its own record, written down as a problem.

The half nobody touched

Now the part that none of the copied policies, none of the taxonomies, and none of the think pieces lay a finger on.

Every gate in this story — 1990s or 2026, problem, person, or provenance — gates entry. Not one of them changes what "done" means.

And before you object: yes, teams verify. QA re-runs the repro steps against the fix and flips the ticket to Verified. Product orgs A/B-test the feature against a metric. Where checking is cheap or the company is rich, evidence exists — about the solution. Does the fix work. Does the metric move. Now ask what closes the problem's record, and watch the trick: on the world's default forum for working together, typing "fixes #123" into a solution closes the problem record automatically, at the instant of merge. Nobody even clicks. The bug tracker of 1998 had a VERIFIED state standing between "fixed" and "closed"; the 2026 default dropped the state and wired closure to shipping. And the problems that aren't reproducible defects — the queue that's too long, the onboarding that confuses, the ecosystem that fragments — have no repro steps to re-run, and in the median team no record at all: nothing was ever written down that could close on evidence.

A problem gate on the front door with "done means shipped" out the back is a bouncer guarding a club with no walls. The full turn has two halves: problems in, disappearance out. Solutions demoted to what they always were — means. If your record can't say which problems are actually gone, your gate is theater with better paperwork.

Now the last exhibit, and it's not who you think — it's me. Our team has been trying problem-first with tooling: typed problem reports, required fields, fixes that must name the problem they answer. The entry half of everything this essay preaches, built and enforced. I suggested the method. I built the tool. I gave the little speeches. And while writing this essay I went looking for our other half — one record, anywhere, of a problem closed because we checked it was gone — and found nothing. We never measured. Done meant merged for us too. I wrote the sermon about walls while proudly polishing a beautiful door. Nobody caught it, including me, until this paragraph.

Shipping a fix and killing a problem are two different events. The one industry that watches whether the problem comes back before closing the record is the one where a regulator fines you for skipping the check. Everyone else verifies the fix, assumes the funeral, and calls it progress.

This was never about software

If this reads like an open-source workflow tip, zoom out. Community organizers have run a hand-operated problem gate since the 1940s: months of "tell me what hurts" conversations before anyone is allowed to propose a campaign. Teachers running improvement cycles start from "what exactly is the problem?" — before any intervention gets airtime. A shop, a school board, a family deciding its rules: anywhere solutions are cheap and attention isn't, the same door works — and generated solutions are about to be cheap everywhere. Software just met the flood first. Your school committee's flood is already on its way.

Steal it

Full disclosure, since this essay argues for disclosure of another kind: I am not neutral, and I am not an expert — my credentials are the confession two sections up. The methodology behind this essay is developed in the open, and this essay is part of that development. The tool I'm building for teams that want the discipline without the paperwork has to solve three recorded problems before it deserves to exist: #1, #2, and #3 — written as problems, gated for solutions. The first person my questions are aimed at is me. Judge the argument, not the author.

The gate itself needs nothing from any of us. It's three sentences in a contribution policy and a template with four required fields — thirty projects already stole it from one terminal emulator, and you can steal it from them. Just do the whole thing, including the part I skipped: type your problems, gate your solutions, and stop closing records that merely shipped.

The problems were always the scarce, human part. The problem gate is your front door finally admitting it.
